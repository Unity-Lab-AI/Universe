# How it works

> One camera, about 42 factors of ten from your horizon to a quark, and on down without a floor. Nothing is swapped in between.

The universe has no edge. What you see ends at a horizon, the distance from which light has had time to reach you, and it is centred on you: move a light-year and you see a light-year further. Galaxies are made wherever you go. The farthest light comes reddened, because the space it crossed was stretching as it came; past the horizon, space stretches away faster than light can cross it, sped by dark energy.

## One camera, infinite zoom

There are no separate views and no loading screens between scales. One camera flies through one universe that holds every scale: the galaxies, each galaxy's stars, each star's system, each world's ground, and the matter it is made of.

Each roll of the wheel moves the camera **a share of the way to the nearest thing**, so the zoom slows by itself as things come close and never runs out.

### No floor

Rolling in, the wheel comes down onto what is there at each size: an atom, then its nucleus, then a proton or neutron, then a quark, and whatever it comes down onto stays in the picture however fast you roll. Inward there is no end. Below the quark, which shows no size down to a ten-thousandth of a femtometre, the zoom keeps going: the scale bar counts down through attometres, zeptometres and yoctometres, past the Planck length, as far as a double-precision number can count (10⁻²⁹⁰ km, which is to say for ever in practice). What is there is what physics knows, which is nothing, and the card says so. Outward you are held inside the visible universe: past its edge there is nothing anyone can see.

## Precision at every scale

A single number can't hold both the width of the universe and a millimetre. So the camera measures itself from **an anchor** close by:

| You are at | The camera measures from |
|---|---|
| Among the galaxies | The nearest galaxy's centre |
| In a star's system | That star |
| At a world | The world itself |
| In the matter | The grain, then the atom |

Everything near you is worked out exactly, whether you are light-years or atoms away. The screen's depth uses *reversed floating point*, so near and far never fight, from a micrometre in front of you to across a galaxy.

## The coefficient waveform (CDF 9/7)

The lab's own wavelet engine, from **Uni Vs Matics**, using the same wavelet as JPEG 2000. A field of values (heights, colours, densities) becomes a short list of wavelet terms:

- **Coarse terms** are the big shapes, **fine terms** the detail.
- The strongest terms are kept until almost all of the field's energy is captured, then packed small.
- A field can be rebuilt at any level of detail, edited by changing a few terms, and saved small.

It carries each world's changes over its six cube faces: mountains wearing down and rising, and new craters.

## Mode-E: detail that never runs out

Where stored detail ends, **mode-E** grows new detail of the same character, for ever. It uses Newton's method on *z³ − K*, carried into a 3D cubic surface and textured by cellular noise whose cells divide by tenfold steps as you zoom.

## The matter, at its real sizes

Below the finest ground, the camera goes on into what the ground is made of, every piece at its real size:

| Layer | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Grains** | About 0.2 mm across, each one mineral from its ground's own mix: quartz, feldspars, clays, olivine, calcite, salt, ices and more |
| **Crystal** | Rock salt as sodium and chlorine on a cube grid 0.282 nm apart, quartz as a silica network, water molecules bent at 104.5° |
| **Atoms** | A grain's atoms are its mineral's, on their real lattice, with the trace elements a real mineral carries each on its real share of sites (nickel in olivine, strontium in feldspar, gold in pyrite, the sea's dissolved ions among its water), so any of the 118 elements can be met where it really is. Each atom's electrons in their real shells, each on a path in its orbital's own shape (s round, p through its two lobes, d round its leaves), a new path every time round and the paths slowly turning besides, so no loop is ever the same twice (an electron has no path of its own, only a cloud of where it may be), round a nucleus about a hundred thousand times smaller than the atom. One drawing at every size: every atom is its electron density, its outer electrons overlapping its neighbours' into the solid's sea of them, an ion's reach as its bond makes it (salt's sodium shrunk, its chlorine swollen), its light kept by its size on screen so a lattice from afar is an even glow; the atom you are at shows its own electrons on their paths and its orbitals on top, and its nucleus a point at the middle once it is big enough. Everything gives off its own light at its temperature, photons of every wavelength from infrared to gamma as Planck's law has them, and one carrying more than an atom's first ionisation energy pulls its outer electron off |
| **Nucleus** | That element's real protons and neutrons |
| **Quarks** | Three in each proton and neutron, held by gluons: points as far as anyone has measured, and the zoom goes on below them without a floor |
| **Small bodies** | An asteroid, a comet's nucleus, a Kuiper body or a rogue planet has its own kind and make-up (an asteroid's by its place in the belt: stony inside, carbon-rich outside, metal, enstatite, basalt and dark icy ones rarer); its card lists its minerals, the elements they hold and the traces down to parts per billion; each is its own size and shape (the smaller the lumpier, round only once gravity has pulled it so), and each is a world like any: within forty of its radii its ground is made as a planet's is, and the way down is the same, its grains, their crystal, its atoms and their quarks |
| **Stars** | Fly into a star and you are in its layers, photosphere to core (convective and radiative zones, a giant's burning shell round its helium core, a supergiant's onion shells down to iron, a white dwarf's carbon and oxygen), each a place to dive into at its own heat, density and make-up. A star's card gives what it is made of by count of atoms, each star with its own share of the heavier elements; its colour and heat come from its mass, not its make-up. Zooming into its surface is its own plasma; into a neutron star, its crust of iron nuclei in a sea of electrons |
| **Black holes** | Every hole, feeding or quiet, shows its photon ring: the thin circle of bent light at the shadow's edge, as the Event Horizon Telescope photographed it, so a quiet hole is a black disc in a ring of light. Inside the horizon the way in to the singularity is millions of light-years for a stellar hole and more for a heavier one, by its mass (`@law hole-inside`), down a funnel fixed in your view whose walls close in as you fall; the card counts the way in. Every hole spins: its horizon pulled in, its ergosphere, space swept round at its horizon, its disc's inner edge where its spin puts it, and space swept round with its spin (frame dragging). Its shadow is black at the size the law gives from where you stand (2.6 horizon radii from afar, half the sky at the photon sphere); the disc's far side arches over it, bent by the hole. Zooming in at the shadow flies down to the horizon, then through it: black, with the gravity well itself round you as a grid, Flamm's surface outside the throat and its inner surface down to the point (both from the metric), the card giving the escape speed (light's at the horizon, more inside), your distance from the middle and your own time left to it, down to the singularity at the quarks' size |
| **Nebulae** | Every galaxy's own, in their real numbers for its kind: emission nebulae of glowing hydrogen and oxygen round their hot young stars, reflection nebulae of dust lit blue, planetary nebulae as thin shells round a white dwarf, supernova remnants as torn filaments, dark clouds of dust and molecules. Each is its own cloud of gas in the colours of its make-up at every distance it can be seen from, made on the graphics card from its own seed, dimming as it shrinks; its stars in it, and its gas dived into like any matter |
| **Supernovae** | Every galaxy has its own, two to five a century, at its own stars, each of a real kind (II-P, II-L, IIn, Ib/Ic, Ia, superluminous) with its real light curve and speed; a going one is as bright as physics says from where you are, up close a fireball ten billion km across in the colour of its heat inside the ballooning cloud of its flung matter; its shell stands for a hundred thousand years, a neutron star, black hole or magnetar is born at its middle, the star that died is gone with its worlds, and its neighbours are scorched |

Click any grain, atom, electron, proton, neutron or quark for its card.

Below light's wavelength (about 400 to 700 nanometres) nothing has a colour, so from there down the grain's face and its stones are shown in greys, as an electron microscope sees them; the colours of the atoms and electrons are marks, not light.

## The four forces, for real

Universe runs on the real laws of nature, with their real numbers (all in `content/physics.unt`):

| Force | What it does here |
|---|---|
| **What the content is** | Every kind of thing is read from the content files, never named in the code; some entries there are words only for now: the design rules and real examples, the descriptions of the climate, rock and forces that are built as the program's own sums, and DNA-AND's life, cities and ships, which come with its game |
| **Climate** | Every world's temperature from its own star's light, its orbit and albedo and its air's greenhouse (Earth 288 K, Venus 736, Mars 212, as measured), warmer at its equator and colder at its poles by the year's sunshine, colder up high by its air's fall of temperature; its air's pressure halving every few km by its own molecules' weight, to its tropopause; its seas salt, warm at the top and near freezing at the floor, frozen over where it is cold, methane and ethane on a world as cold as Titan; their tides from its moons and its star, and their currents from its wind belts. All on the cards |
| **Rivers and lakes** | A world's rain runs downhill to the sea: every basin filled to its spill (a lake), each place draining to its neighbour below, the rivers where enough has gathered, as wide as real rivers of their size |
| **A world at work** | The world you are at has its inside running with time: its heat, its plates or a stagnant lid, its core and magnetic field flipping now and then, its volcanoes, quakes and eruptions, on its card |
| **Gravity** | A live force in the star system you are at: the star and every planet and moon pulling on each other moment by moment (Newton), so orbits precess and tug on each other, started from their Kepler orbits; farther systems keep their Kepler orbits. Every world and moon has its mass, its card the pull at its ground and its escape speed. The Time menu's Gravity switch turns it off: every body, star and galaxy's star goes straight on at the speed it had, every star and gas giant is blown apart by its own pressure on its own free-fall time, a world's air streams off and its seas boil, a black hole's disc and swarm fly apart, belts and rings scatter, and every card says so; turned back on, the system's worlds fall back under their pulls |
| **Electromagnetism** | Electrons fill their shells and orbitals in their real order; bonds are shared electrons; a star's colour comes from its temperature; protons push apart in the nucleus |
| **Strong nuclear force** | Packs protons and neutrons into the nucleus, with a hard core and a short reach; glues quarks into every proton and neutron; sets each nucleus's binding energy |
| **Weak nuclear force** | Decay happens before your eyes: every unstable atom goes at its own moment by its real half-life (a radon atom in days, a uranium atom in billions of years, at whatever speed the clock runs), its alpha knot or electron flung out of it, and it is its daughter from then on. Decides which nuclei are stable and how the rest decay: beta-minus, beta-plus, or alpha past lead |

## A galaxy that fills in round you

A galaxy holds about a hundred billion stars, and the program keeps a sample of 150,000. Round the camera, the real ones are made at the galaxy's true density, in cubes about 25 light-years wide, every cube within about 60 light-years of you (both are traits of the universe template), so the sky is as deep in stars on every side. Each cube is made from its own position, so they are the same stars whenever you come back, and each star is placed exactly, to a fraction of an AU, however far across the galaxy you are.

## Worked out where you are

Anything that would take the whole universe to work out is worked out only where you are, at the detail your view needs, so any one view is easily made:

- **Small streams:** a fine drainage map of the ground round you, forty times your height across, made in the background: streams from about half a square kilometre drained, ponds in the hollows.
- **Ice ages:** each world's ice line from its own climate, swayed by its orbit's cycles (100,000, 405,000, 41,000 and 23,000 years), the ground remade as the ice moves.
- **Galaxies colliding:** the galaxy you are near and its nearest neighbour fall together by their pulls, their stars stepped through both, so the tails, the bridge and the merger come of the physics.
- **Stars born and ageing:** every star round you its own mass and age, through its main sequence, giant and remnant; a nebula's stars switching on as they are born; flares on the star of the system you are at.
- **Seas:** upwelling and the deep water forming, the tide raised in shallows and narrowing bays, each world's own salt from its land, the western boundary currents and the upwellings drawn on the water.
- **Air:** a clear air's sky from its pressure (a thin air darker, a thick one whiter), a dusty air's from its dust.
- **Plates:** the world's ground carried by its plates as they move, raised where they crash and sunk where they spread.
- **Space stretching:** the far galaxies and the cosmic web drawn at their true distances now.
- **Gravity back on:** the stars round you swing back about their paths, swollen stars fall back in on themselves, a black hole's gas and stars fall back onto new orbits round it.

## A living world, even a dead one

Every rocky world runs on its own clock:

- Its heat inside runs down, plates move, volcanoes erupt and quakes shake at real rates.
- The magnetic field flips, and the air and climate follow real chemistry.
- Ice ages come with the orbit's wobbles, mountains wear down, and rocks from space strike.

Its ground is remade at big moments and as its plates and ice move, never piece by piece in front of you.

### Under the sea

A world's seas are dived into. Over water the surface is the floor until you are a metre from it; then the sea bed is, and you go on down through the water: its own colour all round, the light dying away with depth (its star's light is gone within a few hundred metres), everything beyond about thirty metres lost in it, and the bed drawn through it. At the bed the matter is the sea floor's own (silt, sand, its minerals) with water molecules above the grains instead of air, and the zoom goes on into it as on land.

## Time and days

Time runs at the speed you choose, from paused and real time up to a billion years a second (the universe's whole age in fourteen seconds: galaxies turning, space stretching). Every world turns on its own day, so its star rises and sets over the ground. The light on the ground is its star's own colour through its air.

## Your own computer

- The program picks the **strongest graphics card** in the computer.
- The ground is built in the background on **every processor core**, with only what is in view in fine detail and what you need most made first.
- **View > GPU use** sets how much of the card's time it may use, from 10 to 90 per cent. The card's working time is measured every frame and the detail is turned up or down to hold it there.
- **View > Render scale** trades sharpness for speed.
- **View > Show GPU and CPU stats** puts the numbers beside the frames a second: the processor's and the card's time on each frame, the processor's share of every core, the memory in use, the card's own memory in use of what it allows, how busy the card is, the detail, and the ground pieces waiting. They are read twice a second from Windows' own counts, so they cost nothing to show. The same tick is on the landing page.

## Built from scratch

There is no engine under this, no library beside it and no art inside it. The window, the input and the menus are direct calls into Windows. The graphics are Direct3D 12, called through its method tables by our own declarations, with our own shaders compiled as the program starts. The maths (vectors, the CDF 9/7 wavelet, mode-E, the physics), the content format and its reader, every generator (galaxies, stars, systems, worlds, ground, grains, atoms), the camera, the tour, the cards and their blocky font, this document viewer, the installer and the icons are all our own code, the .cdf97w clip format with its wavelet coder and its player too: about 23,000 lines of C# and shader, and 12,000 lines of templates. What it borrows is Windows itself: Direct3D 12 and its shader compiler for the card, the framework's Deflate inside a PNG. The compiler is the one inside every copy of Windows. Nothing is downloaded and nothing is installed to build it.
